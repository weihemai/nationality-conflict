# Introduction

This guide is designed for parents of children who hold—or may hold—both Chinese nationality and one or more other nationalities. The term **国籍冲突 (nationality conflict)** refers to a situation where a child is a Chinese national under Chinese law but also holds a foreign nationality under the law of another country. The term does not appear in the Nationality Law itself [S1]; it is used in Ministry of Public Security (MPS) rules and consular practice [S5][S20].

Because the **《中华人民共和国国籍法》 (Nationality Law of the People's Republic of China)** does not recognize dual nationality for Chinese citizens [S1], families face unique challenges when it comes to birth registration, identity documents, and international travel. This guide walks you through the process from birth to adulthood. Many procedures are set by individual cities or consulates; where a rule comes from one city or one consulate, the guide says so.

# Part One: Understanding the Basics

## 1.1 Key Terms and Definitions

| **English Term** | **Chinese Official Term** | **Meaning** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nationality | 国籍 (*Guójí*) | The legal bond between an individual and a state. China does not recognize dual nationality for Chinese citizens (Nationality Law Art. 3) [S1]. |
| Citizenship | 公民身份 (*Gōngmín Shēnfèn*) | Under Chinese law, synonymous with nationality—all Chinese nationals are Chinese citizens. |
| Household Registration | 户口 (*Hùkǒu*) | The family register that records a person's official place of residence in China. |
| Settled abroad | 定居 (*Dìngjū*) | Used in Nationality Law Art. 5 and Art. 9 [S1]. For children born abroad, consulates assess it by whether the Chinese parent held foreign **permanent residency (永久居留)** at the time of the child's birth [S20][S21][S23]. |
| Nationality determination | 国籍认定 (*Guójí Rèndìng*) | The official decision on whether a person has Chinese nationality—made by the Chinese embassy/consulate abroad, or by the prefecture-level public security exit-entry office in China [S39][S42]. |
| Passport | 护照 (*Hùzhào*) | The document Chinese citizens use to leave and enter China and to prove nationality and identity abroad. Issued by public security exit-entry offices and by Chinese embassies/consulates [S2]. |
| Travel Document | 旅行证 (*Lǚxíngzhèng*) | A passport substitute for Chinese citizens who are inconvenienced by, or do not need, a passport—mainly persons with nationality conflicts [S20][S29]. Not issued by public security offices in mainland China [S20]. |
| Exit-Entry Permit | 出入境通行证 (*Chūrùjìng Tōngxíngzhèng*) | A Chinese travel document issued inside China. For nationality-conflict cases it is valid for 3 months for **one exit and one entry** [S5][S36]. |
| Visa | 签证 (*Qiānzhèng*) | An entry authorization for foreigners. A person whom the consulate determines to be a Chinese national is issued a Travel Document instead of a visa in the foreign passport [S33][S20]. |
| Residence Permit | 外国人居留证件 (*Wàiguórén Jūliú Zhèngjiàn*) | A permit for foreigners staying beyond the period on their visa (non-work permits: 180 days to 5 years) [S3]. Relevant only if the person is determined **not** to be a Chinese national, since the law defines a foreigner as a person without Chinese nationality [S3]. See 7.6. |

## 1.2 The Core Legal Principle

> *"中华人民共和国不承认中国公民具有双重国籍。"* (Nationality Law Art. 3: The People's Republic of China does not recognize dual nationality for any Chinese citizen.) [S1]

When a person has both Chinese nationality and a foreign nationality, **China recognizes only the Chinese nationality**. The National Immigration Administration (NIA) has confirmed that China does not recognize the foreign nationality of a person who is Chinese under the law [S12]. Chinese embassies and consulates therefore **do not issue a Chinese visa** in such a person's foreign passport; they issue a **Travel Document** instead [S33]. Parents cannot choose between a visa and a Travel Document: the consulate decides, based on the place of birth, the child's foreign nationality and the parents' residence status at the time of birth [S20].

## 1.3 How Nationality Is Determined Under Chinese Law

The **《中华人民共和国国籍法》 (Nationality Law of the PRC)** sets out the following rules [S1]:

- **Article 3**: The PRC does not recognize dual nationality for Chinese citizens.
- **Article 4**: A person born in China with at least one Chinese-national parent has Chinese nationality.
- **Article 5**: A person born abroad with at least one Chinese-national parent has Chinese nationality—**except** where the child acquired a foreign nationality at birth **and** the Chinese-national parent(s) had **settled abroad**.
- **Article 9**: Chinese citizens who **settle abroad** and **voluntarily acquire foreign nationality** automatically lose Chinese nationality.

**When both parents are Chinese, one parent's settlement is enough.** A 2008 MPS/MFA notice on Article 5 (公境〔2008〕2204号) lists three situations in which a child who is foreign at birth does **not** have Chinese nationality: (1) both parents are Chinese and both have settled abroad; (2) one parent is foreign and the Chinese parent has settled abroad; (3) **both parents are Chinese and either one of them has settled abroad** [S6]. The notice's full text was not found on an official website, but the same outcome appears in the UK Embassy's reference table and the Los Angeles Consulate's eligibility rules [S21][S23].

The MFA stresses that nationality cannot be inferred from the parents' nationality alone: their settlement status and the place of birth also count, so children in the same family can end up with different nationalities [S18].

**For parents, this means (status at the time of the child's birth):**

| **Scenario** | **Chinese nationality?** | **Document for travel to China** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Born in China, at least one Chinese parent | ✅ Yes (Art. 4); nationality conflict if the child also has a foreign nationality [S1][S21] | *Hukou* and passport, or Exit-Entry Permit in China; Travel Document abroad [S21][S23] |
| Born abroad, **both** parents Chinese, **neither** held foreign permanent residency; child foreign at birth | ✅ Yes — nationality conflict [S20][S23] | Travel Document [S23] |
| Born abroad, one Chinese parent **without** foreign permanent residency, the other parent foreign; child foreign at birth | ✅ Yes — nationality conflict [S20][S23] | Travel Document [S23] |
| Born abroad, both parents Chinese, **either one** held foreign permanent residency; child foreign at birth | ❌ No [S6][S21] | Visa [S21] |
| Born abroad, Chinese parent held foreign permanent residency, the other parent foreign; child foreign at birth | ❌ No [S6][S21] | Visa [S21] |
| Born abroad, child did **not** acquire a foreign nationality at birth | ✅ Yes — no conflict [S1][S21] | Chinese passport [S21] |
| Chinese citizen settles abroad and voluntarily acquires foreign nationality | ❌ Automatically loses Chinese nationality (Art. 9) [S1] | Visa [S21] |

Practice has varied between countries and over time. The determination is made by the consulate handling the application [S20].

# Part Two: Birth and Early Years

## 2.1 Before Birth: What to Consider

If you are a Chinese citizen living abroad or in a cross-national marriage (跨国婚姻), consider:

1. **Where will the child be born?** The place of birth affects whether the child acquires a foreign nationality at birth.
2. **Does either Chinese parent hold foreign permanent residency?** If either Chinese parent holds it at the time of birth and the child acquires a foreign nationality at birth, the child generally does **not** have Chinese nationality [S6][S21].
3. **What is the Chinese parents' legal status in the country of birth?** Consulates ask for the residence permits or visas the parents held at the birth. The Jamaica Embassy says it cannot issue a Travel Document if the Chinese parents have no legal status there or their status cannot be established [S20][S23].
4. **Where will the child primarily reside?** This affects which travel documents will be needed.

## 2.2 At Birth: Nationality Determination

**For children born abroad:**
> *"父母双方或一方为中国公民，本人出生在外国，具有中国国籍；但父母双方或一方为中国公民并定居在外国，本人出生时即具有外国国籍的，不具有中国国籍。"* (Nationality Law Art. 5) [S1]

**Translation**: A child born abroad to one or two Chinese-national parents has Chinese nationality—**unless** the child had a foreign nationality at birth **and** the Chinese-national parent(s) had settled abroad (in consular practice: held foreign permanent residency) at the time of birth. If both parents are Chinese, it is enough that **one** of them had settled abroad [S6][S21].

**For children born in China to one foreign parent:**
> *"在中国出生，父母一方为中国公民，另一方为外国公民，且按照外籍父母国籍国法律，出生即具有外国国籍的儿童"*

Such children have Chinese nationality under Article 4 but may also have a foreign nationality—creating a **nationality conflict**. (The exact origin of this quotation could not be located; the same category appears in the Los Angeles Consulate's eligibility list [S23].)

## 2.3 The Birth Certificate: Name Requirements

### If the child is born in China

- Local implementing rules for the **《出生医学证明》 (Medical Certificate of Birth)** require the characters of the given name to come from the **《通用规范汉字表》 (Table of General Standard Chinese Characters, 2013)** [S44].
- If one or both parents are foreign, the name field may be completed in **Chinese or English** [S44].
- For *hukou* registration the name must use standard Chinese characters. Guangdong allows the birth certificate to be **reissued (换发)** when the public security bureau issues a letter stating that birth registration cannot proceed, including where the name uses non-standard characters [S43]. Reissue rules vary by province, so check with the local health commission before choosing an English-only name if you may register *hukou* later.
- **Shanghai example**: Shanghai's rules allow some details of foreign nationals on the certificate to be printed in English, and provide for a reissue with a changed newborn name when the *hukou* registration office certifies that birth registration cannot proceed under the original name [S48].

### If the child is born abroad

- Travel Document applications record both the child's Chinese and foreign-language names (e.g., the Los Angeles declaration asks for both) [S24].
- A Chinese **passport** holder can apply for an endorsement (加注) of a foreign-language name, a former name or a traditional-character name (MPS measures Art. 9) [S4]. Travel Documents also have an observation page (备注页) for endorsements [S28]; ask the consulate whether a foreign-name endorsement is available.
- For *hukou* registration in China, the foreign birth certificate needs a Chinese translation by a qualified translation agency **and** authentication: consular legalization, an **apostille** (for Apostille Convention states, since 7 November 2023), or a notarial certificate, depending on local rules [S19][S42][S37]. See 7.4.

### Name Change After Registration

- **Legal basis**: under the Hukou Registration Regulations (1958) Art. 18, a person under 18 changes a name through themselves or their parents/adoptive parents; a person aged 18 or over applies personally [S8].
- **Local conditions apply.** Example: Zhejiang requires both parents to agree to a minor's name change and, for a child aged 10 or older, the child's own consent [S45]. Other localities may set different conditions.

# Part Three: Household Registration (Hukou)

## 3.1 What Is Hukou and Why Does It Matter?

**户口 (*Hukou*)** is the official family register in China. It records a person's identity, place of residence, and family relationships. Having *hukou* is important for access to education, healthcare, and many other services in China.

Children with nationality conflicts are Chinese citizens and receive national treatment in China, but local *hukou* policies differ, so requirements vary by city [S20].

**Important — what *hukou* does and does not mean for nationality:**

- *Hukou* is a record of where a person is registered as living in China. It is not a nationality decision and not a travel document.
- Registering *hukou* does **not** end the nationality conflict. The other country still treats the person as its national under its own law, China still does not recognize that nationality, and the person still needs Chinese documents to enter and leave China (Parts Four and Five).
- Having *hukou* is consistent with holding Chinese nationality — registration presupposes it, and the public security bureau may require a nationality determination before registering (see 3.3) [S39][S42].
- **Not** having *hukou* does not mean the person lacks Chinese nationality. Nationality-conflict children living abroad usually have no *hukou* at all.
- Chinese nationality is lost only under Art. 9 or Art. 11 of the Nationality Law (see 6.1) [S1]. That is a separate matter from *hukou*, though someone who has lost Chinese nationality must then apply to cancel their *hukou* and hand back the Resident ID Card (Shanghai rules) [S46].

## 3.2 Registering Hukou for a Child Born Abroad

Rules are set locally. The examples below are from Tianjin and Fuzhou; check the rules of the city where you will register.

**Tianjin example** [S40]:

- **Eligibility**: The child has Chinese nationality, at least one parent is a Chinese citizen with Tianjin *hukou*, and the child entered China with a Chinese passport or Travel Document.
- **Who applies**: The father or mother in person (no proxies), at the police station where the parent's *hukou* is registered.
- **Materials**: 《国外出生户口登记承诺书》 (registration undertaking); the foreign birth certificate; the passport or Travel Document that the child and the Chinese parent(s) used for their last entry; the parents' marriage certificate; the Chinese parent(s)' Resident ID Card; the 《居民户口簿》 (Household Register) of the household the child joins.
- **Under 6**: Community police check; approved by the head of the police station.
- **Aged 6 to under 18**:

> *"年满6周岁不满18周岁的，还应当提交亲子鉴定证明，逐级报公安分局审核后办理。"*

**Translation**: For children aged 6 to under 18, a **亲子鉴定证明 (parentage/DNA test certificate)** must also be submitted, and the application is reviewed level by level up to the district Public Security Sub-bureau.

- **Doubtful nationality**: The municipal exit-entry corps makes a nationality determination.

**Fuzhou example** [S42] — the child is registered under the rules for persons who are not overseas Chinese (非华侨). Materials include:

- a 《国籍认定意见》 (nationality determination opinion), where required (see 3.3);
- the parents' travel documents used to return to China, their Household Register(s), Resident ID Cards and marriage certificate;
- the foreign birth certificate with a translation by a qualified agency, plus consular legalization, a treaty-recognized certificate (apostille) or a notarial certificate;
- **a declaration or proof that the child has not acquired, or has given up, foreign citizenship or foreign permanent residency**.

If the child leaves China before the registration is approved, the application must be filed again [S42].

**Shanghai example** [S46][S47]:

- **Born in China**: registration uses the 《出生医学证明》. A parentage (DNA) report is **not** required as a rule; Shanghai lists the cases in which it is, e.g., no valid birth certificate, parents' details that differ from the certificate, birth outside a midwifery institution, a foreign birth certificate not legalized by the Chinese embassy/consulate, doubtful records, or implausible birth circumstances. There is no age-based DNA requirement like Tianjin's.
- **Born abroad**: a child **under 16** can be registered at the police station of the parent's Shanghai *hukou* if the child **has not acquired, or has given up, foreign nationality** and entered China with a Chinese passport, Travel Document or Exit-Entry Permit; from age 16 the rules for overseas Chinese settling in Shanghai apply (Shanghai Hukou Rules Art. 22) [S46].
- **Foreign documents**: legalized by the Chinese embassy/consulate or notarized; from Apostille Convention states, an apostille; non-Chinese documents need a translation by a qualified Shanghai translation agency [S47].

**Children of overseas Chinese (华侨)**: Tianjin's separate procedure additionally requires, for ages 6 to under 18, a comparison against the national anti-trafficking DNA database, unless parentage has been confirmed by DNA testing [S41].

## 3.3 Nationality Determination for Hukou Registration

If the child's nationality status is unclear (e.g., the child entered with a foreign passport), the public security bureau may require:

> *"持外国护照入境或申请人国籍情况存有疑义的，经设区市（含）以上公安机关出入境管理部门认定具有中国国籍的"* [S42]

**Translation**: If the child entered with a foreign passport or the nationality status is in doubt, the exit-entry administration at the prefecture level or above must first **determine that the child has Chinese nationality** before *hukou* registration can proceed.

The Guangdong Public Security Department describes the general approach: a child with a nationality conflict needs a nationality determination—from the Chinese embassy/consulate if abroad, or from the prefecture-level exit-entry office where a parent has *hukou* or lives if in China. A child found to be Chinese can register *hukou* or obtain an Exit-Entry Permit in China, or a Travel Document abroad; a child found to be foreign is managed as a foreigner (visa or residence permit) [S39].

## 3.4 What If the Child Already Has Hukou?

If the child already has *hukou* in China, this does **not** end the nationality conflict. A child with *hukou* may also hold a Chinese passport (see 4.2) [S25][S21].

Some cities' registration rules ask for a declaration that the child has not acquired, or has given up, foreign citizenship or foreign permanent residency (e.g., Fuzhou) [S42]. Ask the registration office how a child who already holds a foreign passport will be treated before applying.

# Part Four: Identity Documents

## 4.1 Overview: Which Document for Which Situation?

| **Document** | **Issuing Authority** | **Validity** | **When to Use** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Chinese Passport** (普通护照) | Public security exit-entry offices; Chinese embassies/consulates [S2] | 5 years if the holder is under 16; 10 years if 16 or older (Passport Law Art. 7) [S2] | Chinese citizens. A nationality-conflict person with *hukou* may also hold one (see 4.2) [S25][S21] |
| **Travel Document** (旅行证) | Chinese embassies/consulates and the MFA offices in Hong Kong and Macau; **not** issued by public security offices in mainland China [S20] | Generally 2 years with multiple exits and entries, unless endorsed for a single trip or for return only [S10][S29] | **Nationality-conflict persons outside China** [S20][S23] |
| **Exit-Entry Permit** (出入境通行证) | Prefecture-level (设区市) public security exit-entry office where the applicant lives [S5][S36] | 3 months, **one exit and one entry** [S5][S36][S37][S38] | **Nationality-conflict persons inside China** without another usable Chinese document [S5][S9] |

## 4.2 Can a Person with Nationality Conflict Get a Chinese Passport?

**Yes. There is no legal bar, but practice differs between consulates and localities.**

- **Legal position.** A nationality-conflict person is a Chinese national. The refusal ground in the Passport Law and the MPS measures, *"不具有中华人民共和国国籍的"* (does not have Chinese nationality), applies to non-nationals, so it does not bar them [S2][S4]. The Exit-Entry Permit exists for those who find it **inconvenient (不便)** to use an ordinary passport [S5][S36]. It is an alternative, not a prohibition.
- **Nagoya Consulate-General (June 2025):** passports and Travel Documents are both issued to Chinese citizens and do not change the holder's nationality status. A child who obtained a Chinese passport after returning to China with a Travel Document need not renew the Travel Document, and one person should not hold both at the same time [S25].
- **UK Embassy:** China-born dual nationals who registered *hukou* and obtained a Chinese passport have the passport renewed; those without *hukou* are issued Travel Documents [S21].
- **Abroad**, consulates generally issue Travel Documents rather than first-time passports to nationality-conflict persons. For example, Lahore describes first-time passports as mainly for people born abroad without a nationality conflict [S31][S25].

A Chinese passport does not by itself give the right to enter the country of the other nationality; see 4.5, 5.6, 7.2 and 7.3.

## 4.3 Applying for a Travel Document (Overseas)

**Who qualifies?**
> *"事实上存在国籍冲突的人员"* (Persons with de facto nationality conflicts) [S32][S28]

**Specific categories** (Los Angeles Consulate wording [S23]):

1. Born abroad with a foreign nationality at birth; **both parents Chinese and neither** held foreign permanent residency at the time of birth.
2. Born abroad with a foreign nationality at birth; **one parent Chinese without** foreign permanent residency at the time of birth, the other parent foreign.
3. Born in China to one Chinese and one foreign parent, with a foreign nationality at birth under the foreign parent's nationality law.

If either Chinese parent held foreign permanent residency at the time of birth, the child does not qualify and applies for a visa instead [S6][S21][S23].

**Where to apply**

- **First application**: generally at the Chinese embassy/consulate in the child's **country of birth** [S20][S27]. The Kuwait Embassy accepts other cases only after consulting the post in the birth country [S27].
- **Renewals and replacements**: at the embassy/consulate of the country where the person now lives, or at the MFA offices in Hong Kong and Macau [S20][S17].
- **[Practice report] First issue in Hong Kong**: families report recently obtaining a **first** Travel Document directly from the MFA Office in Hong Kong. The Office's published scope does not cover this: it lists Travel Document **renewal** and **replacement** (both of which require an existing document), plus a one-trip Travel Document for Chinese citizens temporarily in Hong Kong who are stranded without valid documents [S59]. Practice may be ahead of the published notice, so confirm by email (fmcozgzj_hk@mfa.gov.cn) or phone (+852 3413 2404) before travelling to Hong Kong for this purpose [S59].
- Some posts (e.g., Los Angeles) require the applicant to be physically inside their consular district [S23].

**Application process**

- Submit through the **"中国领事" APP (China Consular Affairs APP)** and upload the documents [S20][S23].
- The APP runs **face recognition** on applicants; minors may be called to a video interview together with a parent [S23].
- Appear in person or mail the originals when instructed [S23].

**Typical first-issue materials** (Los Angeles [S23]; similar lists at Kota Kinabalu [S30], Kuching [S35], Jordan [S26] and Kuwait [S27]):

- Recent photo (white background).
- The child's birth certificate (or other proof of parentage).
- Both parents' valid passports.
- The residence permits or visas each parent held **at the time of the child's birth** (children born abroad).
- Household Register or Resident ID Card, if any (children born in China).
- The child's foreign passport, if any [S27].
- For applicants **under 16**: one parent or legal guardian attends (or joins the video interview) and gives written consent [S26][S23][S30].
- Los Angeles: both parents sign Part 1 of the 《国籍冲突人员办理旅行证声明书》, stating their nationality and that they held no foreign permanent residency at the time of birth [S24].
- Proof of address in the consular district (Los Angeles) [S23].

**Processing**: Los Angeles normally issues within 4 working days after the documents arrive, longer when identity or nationality must be checked [S23].

**Renewal (换发)**: Los Angeles renews when the Travel Document has expired, has less than 6 months left, has fewer than 4 blank visa pages, or when the holder left China on an Exit-Entry Permit [S23]. Denmark renews mainly when less than 6 months remain [S28].

**Using the Travel Document**: the holder enters China as a Chinese citizen without a visa [S20]. It is intended mainly for travel to China; whether it can be used for third countries depends on the destination country [S20][S29].

**What the "6 months" figure means**: many countries require a *passport* to have at least 6 months of validity left before you may travel. The 6 months that appears in Chinese consular guidance is a different thing — it is the point from which you become **eligible to apply for a new Travel Document**, not a minimum you must have left in order to travel. Los Angeles, Denmark and the Hong Kong office all use "expired, or less than 6 months remaining" as a renewal trigger [S23][S28][S59]. No Chinese source was found that sets a minimum remaining validity for entering or leaving China on a Travel Document. Airlines and third countries may still apply their own rules, so allow margin if a trip falls close to the expiry date.

## 4.4 Applying for an Exit-Entry Permit (Domestic)

**Legal basis**: Article 16 of the MPS 《中华人民共和国出入境通行证签发管理工作规范》 (公境出〔2007〕2223号), an internal normative document quoted in official service listings [S5]. The NIA's national service guide also lists nationality conflict as a ground for the permit [S9]. The published MPS Order No. 96, 《普通护照和出入境通行证签发管理办法》, covers exit-entry permits only for border trade and border tourism and does not mention nationality conflict [S4].

**Who qualifies?**
> *"申请人因国籍冲突，不便持用普通护照的"* (Applicants who, due to nationality conflicts, are inconvenienced to hold ordinary passports) [S5]

**Where**: the prefecture-level (设区市) public security exit-entry office of the place of residence [S5][S36].

**Standard issuance**: a 3-month permit valid for **one exit and one entry** (三个月一次出入境有效) [S5][S36][S37][S38].

**Required materials** (vary by city):

- **Fujian** (baseline) [S36]: application form; photo; Resident ID Card (applicants with *hukou*), or the Household Register for under-16s without an ID card; the foreign passport and a copy. Materials supporting the reason for travel may be requested.
- **Dongguan** (longer list) [S37]:
  - Completed application form with photo
  - Birth certificate
  - The foreign passport held by the applicant
  - Both parents' identity documents and proof that one parent lives in Dongguan (e.g., Dongguan residence permit); not needed if a parent has Dongguan *hukou*
  - If entered using a Travel Document: the Travel Document
  - Copy of the latest entry stamp
  - If renewing: the original Exit-Entry Permit
  - If born abroad to Chinese parent(s): the parents' Chinese passports and the foreign visas/residence permits they held at the time of birth
  - Proof of travel to the country of the other nationality (e.g., flight ticket)
  - If *hukou* is registered: Resident ID Card and Household Register
  - Marriage certificate (if applicable)
  - Foreign documents must be authenticated and translated by a qualified agency

**Who applies**

- **Fujian**: the applicant in person; under-16s are accompanied by a guardian with proof of guardianship, or by an authorized companion holding the guardian's power of attorney [S36].
- **Dongguan**: for applicants under 18, a guardian may apply on their behalf [S37].

**Processing time**: Fujian 7 working days (5 if urgent) [S36]; Dongguan 5 working days (3 if urgent) [S37]; Hangzhou 7 working days (statutory limit 15) [S38].

**Fee**: RMB 15 per permit (fee basis: 发改价格〔2017〕1186号) [S36][S37].

## 4.5 Comparing the Three Documents

| | **Exit-Entry Permit** | **Travel Document** | **Chinese Passport** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where issued | Mainland China only (prefecture-level PSB) [S5] | Outside mainland China only; renewals also at the MFA offices in Hong Kong/Macau [S20][S59] | Mainland China or abroad [S2] |
| Typical processing | 5–7 working days [S36][S37] | Hong Kong: 1–3 working days, no same-day service [S59]; abroad by mail: longer (see below) | Shanghai renewal: 7 working days [S52] |
| Validity | 3 months [S5] | Generally 2 years [S10] | 5 or 10 years [S2] |
| Trips | One exit and one entry [S5] | Multiple, unless endorsed [S10] | Multiple |
| Leaving for the other-nationality country | Yes; Dongguan issues it for travel to that country [S37] | Yes (see below) | Needs a destination visa [S3], which that country normally does not issue to its own citizens [S63] |
| Can be held together with | A passport [S21] | Not with a valid Chinese passport [S25][S58] | Not with a Travel Document [S25][S58] |

- **Exit-Entry Permit**: quick to obtain locally, but only one exit and one entry within 3 months, so every trip needs a new application.
- **Travel Document**: works much like a passport, but only for leaving and entering China. **[Practice report]** At Chinese exit control, holders are generally not asked for a visa for the destination country. It cannot be obtained inside mainland China. In Hong Kong the current notice gives collection after 1, 2 or 3 working days depending on the service level, with no same-day option; the application must be filed in person by appointment and the old document is kept until collection, so the applicant has to wait in Hong Kong or pay for local return by courier [S59]. **[Practice report]** Mail-in applications at consulates abroad can take 3–4 weeks door to door; Los Angeles quotes 4 working days after the documents arrive [S23].
- **Chinese passport**: full Chinese-citizen documentation, but leaving China requires a visa or other entry permission for the destination (Exit-Entry Law Art. 9) [S3]. Home countries normally do not issue visas to their own citizens (US example [S63]), so an Exit-Entry Permit—or a pro forma visa (7.3)—is usually still needed. One person should not hold a passport and a Travel Document at the same time; the Hong Kong office does not issue a Travel Document to someone holding a valid Chinese passport [S25][S58].

# Part Five: Travel Scenarios and Requirements

## 5.1 Core Principle for Border Crossing

Chinese citizens leave and enter China with a passport or another Chinese travel document, and need a visa or other entry permission for the destination unless an exemption applies (Exit-Entry Law Art. 9) [S3]. They present these documents to border inspection (Art. 11), and a person without valid exit-entry documents is not allowed to leave (Art. 12) [S3].

**Key rule**: If you are determined to be a Chinese national, you use Chinese documents (Passport, Travel Document, or Exit-Entry Permit) to enter and leave China. Visas are for foreigners, and a consulate that determines you are Chinese issues a Travel Document instead [S3][S20][S33].

## 5.2 Scenario 1: Child Resides Outside China, Traveling to China

**Document needed**: **Travel Document** (旅行证)

- Validity: generally 2 years, multiple entries, unless endorsed otherwise [S10]
- First application: usually at the embassy/consulate in the country of birth [S20]
- The child enters China as a Chinese citizen—no visa required [S20]

**If the Travel Document expires while in China**:

- Expiry does not generally affect living or studying in China [S20].
- To leave, apply to the local public security exit-entry office for a one-time Exit-Entry Permit, then renew the Travel Document at a Chinese embassy/consulate abroad [S17][S20].

## 5.3 Scenario 2: Child Resides in China, Traveling Abroad

- **Valid Travel Document**: use it; it allows multiple exits and entries within its validity [S10].
- **Hukou and a Chinese passport**: the passport may be used (see 4.2) [S25]. The UK Embassy notes that such persons have also left China on a one-time Exit-Entry Permit [S21]; see 4.5 and 7.3.
- **Otherwise**: **Exit-Entry Permit** (出入境通行证)
  - Validity: 3 months, **one exit and one entry** [S5][S36]
  - Apply at the prefecture-level public security exit-entry office where you live [S5]
  - Each trip needs a new application. The MPS rule provides only this 3-month permit for nationality-conflict cases [S5]; the multi-entry permits in the MPS measures are for border trade and border tourism [S4].

**Coming back**:

- Returning **within** the permit's 3-month validity: the same permit covers the return entry [S5][S36].
- Staying abroad **longer**: apply for a Travel Document at the Chinese embassy/consulate abroad. Los Angeles treats a person who left China on an Exit-Entry Permit as a Travel Document renewal case [S23].
- The MFA and the Jamaica Embassy describe the permit issued after a Travel Document expires as a one-time document for leaving China [S17][S20]. Always check the validity endorsed on the permit itself.

## 5.4 Scenario 3: Travel Document Expired While in China

**[Unverified]** The previous version quoted this NIA reply (source [S13]; the reply could not be located on nia.gov.cn):

> *"若您的孩子是持我驻外使领馆签发的旅行证回国，那您的孩子应是具有中国国籍，若其因国籍冲突，不便持用普通护照，可前往当地公安机关出入境管理部门申请三个月一次出境有效的出入境通行证。"*

**Translation**: If your child entered China with a Travel Document issued by a Chinese embassy/consulate, the child has Chinese nationality. If the child is inconvenienced to hold an ordinary passport due to nationality conflict, you may apply to the local public security exit-entry administration for a 3-month Exit-Entry Permit valid for one exit.

This wording ("one exit") differs from the MPS rule ("one exit and one entry") [S5].

**Verified guidance**: the MFA and the Jamaica Embassy advise applying to the local public security office for a one-time Exit-Entry Permit to leave, then renewing the Travel Document abroad—at a Chinese embassy/consulate in the country where the child lives, or at the MFA offices in Hong Kong/Macau [S17][S20].

## 5.5 Scenario 4: Foreign Passport plus Chinese Visa?

**This is not available.** The consulate decides whether a child receives a visa or a Travel Document, based on the place of birth, the foreign nationality and the parents' residence status at the time of birth; parents cannot choose [S20]. A person the consulate determines to be Chinese is issued a Travel Document, not a visa in the foreign passport [S33][S20].

## 5.6 Scenario 5: Traveling Between China and the US, Canada, Australia or New Zealand

China's consular treaties with the **United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand** contain a clause facilitating travel between the two countries for persons who claim both nationalities, without recognizing dual nationality [S25]. The China–US exchange of notes (17 September 1980) specifies that exit procedures and documents follow the law of the country of residence, and entry procedures and documents follow the law of the destination country [S7]. See 7.2. No comparable clause was found for other countries (e.g., France). Some families also use pro forma visas or right-of-abode certificates; see 7.3.

## 5.7 Scenario 6: Visa-Free Entry with the Foreign Passport?

**Caveat: no official statement specifically addressing nationality-conflict persons was found.** The following is derived from the law:

- The Exit-Entry Law defines a foreigner as a person without Chinese nationality—*"外国人，是指不具有中国国籍的人"* (Art. 89)—and its visa exemptions (Art. 22) apply to foreigners [S3].
- Embassy FAQs describe China's unilateral visa-free policy as applying to foreigners holding ordinary passports [S34].
- Chinese citizens must leave and enter China with a passport or other Chinese travel document (Art. 9) [S3].

A person China regards as a Chinese national should therefore not expect to use visa-free or transit visa-free entry with a foreign passport. Confirm with the consulate, or with the local exit-entry office (hotline 12367), before travelling [S37].

**[Practice report]** Some young adults who grew up in China registered only as foreigners (never with *hukou*, only with residence permits in the foreign passport, see 7.6) have later, after moving abroad for university, entered China under the visa-free arrangements for EU countries. This does not change the legal position above: nationality is determined when documents are applied for [S11][S12], and a later determination could still treat the person as Chinese. For French passport holders, the French government lists the visa exemption (stays of up to 30 days) as valid until 31 December 2026 [S64].

# Part Six: Adulthood (Age 18 and Above)

## 6.1 Does Nationality Conflict Automatically End at 18?

**No.** The nationality conflict does **not** end automatically at 18.

The **National Immigration Administration (国家移民管理局)** replied [S11]:
> *"相关子女向中国主管部门申请办理出入境证件时，有关部门将会根据具体情况对其国籍进行认定。"*

**Translation**: When such children apply to Chinese authorities for exit-entry documents, the relevant departments will determine their nationality based on the specific circumstances.

- In another reply, the NIA said that China does not recognize the foreign nationality of a person who is Chinese under the law, and that the exit-entry office determines the person's nationality before issuing the corresponding documents [S12].
- The Jamaica Embassy states that nationality-conflict children who applied for and used Travel Documents continue to do so as adults [S20].
- Asked whether a Travel Document becomes invalid when the holder turns 18, the NIA replied only with the general rule: usually valid for 2 years with multiple exits and entries, unless endorsed otherwise [S10].

**Two ways to lose Chinese nationality** [S1][S11]:

1. **Article 9 of the Nationality Law**: Settle abroad and voluntarily acquire foreign nationality → automatic loss
2. **Article 11 of the Nationality Law**: Apply to renounce Chinese nationality and receive approval

If **neither** applies, the person **retains Chinese nationality** and continues to use Chinese documents (Passport, Travel Document or Exit-Entry Permit).

**Consular practice differs.** The Edinburgh Consulate-General states that a child who had Chinese nationality at birth automatically loses it if the child later settles abroad while holding a foreign nationality [S22]. The UK Embassy's reference table likewise distinguishes later naturalization with foreign permanent residency (visa) from naturalization without it (Travel Document) [S21]. Check with the consulate for your district.

## 6.2 Can an Adult Apply for a Travel Document?

**Yes.** The Los Angeles Consulate has a category for first-time applicants aged **16 or over** who have never held a Chinese travel document, using the same parentage criteria as for children [S23]. Its declaration form covers:
> *"申请人为未满16周岁的未成年人 ... 或首次办理的成年人"* (Applicants who are minors under 16 ... or adults applying for the first time) [S24]

**For adults applying for a Travel Document**:

- Apply personally; no guardian accompaniment.
- For a **first** application, the parents still complete Part 1 of the declaration, covering their nationality and residence status at the time of birth [S24]. Applicants aged 16 or over who are renewing complete Part 2 themselves [S23].
- Face recognition in the APP applies to all applicants [S23].
- **[Unverified]** Upload of entry-exit records from the NIA mini-program. The previous version attributed this to the Calgary Consulate; it was not found on the Calgary or Los Angeles pages checked.

## 6.3 Can an Adult Apply for an Exit-Entry Permit?

**Yes.** The MPS rule sets no age condition [S5]. In Fujian, applicants aged 16 or over apply in person; under-16s are accompanied by a guardian [S36].

## 6.4 What If the Adult Has Hukou?

*Hukou* does not by itself change nationality status. A person with *hukou* may hold a Chinese passport (see 4.2) [S25][S21]; otherwise the person continues to use a Travel Document abroad or an Exit-Entry Permit in China. Fujian's permit guide lists the Resident ID Card among the materials for applicants with *hukou*, so having *hukou* does not exclude the permit [S36].

## 6.5 Can an Adult Renounce Chinese Nationality?

**Yes**, under certain conditions.

**《国籍法》第十条 (Nationality Law Article 10)** [S1]: A Chinese citizen may apply to renounce Chinese nationality if they meet **one** of the following conditions:

1. A close relative who is a foreign national
2. Settled abroad
3. Other legitimate reasons

**Who cannot renounce** (Article 12) [S1]:
> *"国家工作人员和现役军人，不得退出中国国籍。"* (State functionaries and active-duty military personnel shall not renounce Chinese nationality.)

**Procedure** [S1]:

- **Minors**: applications for persons under 18 **may** be filed by their parents or other legal representatives (Art. 14).
- **Where**: in China, the local city or county public security bureau; abroad, a Chinese embassy or consulate (Art. 15).
- **Approval**: by the Ministry of Public Security, which issues a certificate (Art. 16). Nationality is lost once the application is approved (Art. 11).
- **Afterwards**: return any Chinese passport or Exit-Entry Permit to the public security office or a Chinese embassy/consulate [S4], and travel to China with a foreign passport and a Chinese visa [S21].
- **Staying on after approval**: Shanghai lists a foreigner's stay permit (外国人停留证件) for people approved to renounce who need to remain in China [S51].

**Minors in practice**:

- National law lets parents or other legal representatives apply for under-18s (Art. 14) [S1], and official guides accept minors. Dongguan first confirms that a minor holding a foreign passport is Chinese before accepting the application [S60]; the Singapore Embassy requires a legal guardian to accompany an applicant under 18 [S61]. In China, the NIA says to apply at the exit-entry office of the place of *hukou* [S57].
- **[Practice report] (second-hand) Shanghai**: other families report that renunciation is not possible before age 18. No written Shanghai rule to this effect was found, and no first-hand account of a refusal was available, so treat this as a rumour to check rather than a rule: national law and other cities' guides do accept applications for minors [S1][S60][S61]. A 2017 French Senate question reported that local administrations told Franco-Chinese families they did not know the procedure for giving up Chinese nationality, which is consistent with inconsistent local handling rather than a fixed age limit [S62]. Before planning around this, ask the Shanghai exit-entry authority directly (12367).
- This matters for permanent residence, which children can obtain through a parent only until 18 (see 6.6).

## 6.6 Chinese Permanent Residence ("China Green Card") for Children

- **Only for foreigners**: permanent residence is a status for foreigners (Exit-Entry Law Art. 47), and a foreigner is a person without Chinese nationality (Art. 89) [S3]. A child whom China regards as Chinese must first lose Chinese nationality, in practice by renunciation (6.5).
- **Parent-child category (亲子团聚)**: unmarried children under 18 joining their parents are one of the national categories [S53]. Shanghai's guide requires one or both parents to be Chinese citizens with Chinese *hukou* (or foreigners with Chinese permanent residence), and the child to be under 18, unmarried and **confirmed by a nationality review to have foreign nationality** [S49].
- **Where to apply**: the exit-entry office of the prefecture-level city (in municipalities, the district or county public security bureau) [S53]. In Shanghai: the Municipal Immigration Service Center or a district exit-entry office; the review takes up to 90 working days before the file goes to the NIA [S49]. **[Practice report]** The application follows the Chinese parent and is made where that parent's *hukou* is registered.
- **Timing risk**: because the category ends at 18, a child who cannot renounce before 18 (reported Shanghai practice, 6.5) would no longer qualify by the time renunciation becomes possible.

# Part Seven: Special Considerations

## 7.1 Hukou and Exit-Entry Permits

**[Unverified]** The previous version quoted this NIA reply (source [S14]; the reply could not be located on nia.gov.cn):
> *"我国公民因国籍冲突不便于持用普通护照时，可以申请办理中华人民共和国出入境通行证，与是否需注销户籍无关。"*

**Translation**: Chinese citizens who are inconvenienced to hold ordinary passports due to nationality conflicts may apply for a PRC Exit-Entry Permit, which is **unrelated to whether hukou needs to be cancelled**.

**Verified points**:

- The MPS rule for the permit sets no *hukou* condition [S5].
- Fujian's guide lists the Resident ID Card among the materials for applicants who have *hukou* [S36], and Dongguan's lists the ID card and Household Register for applicants whose *hukou* is registered [S37].
- The Guangdong Public Security Department says a child determined to be Chinese can register *hukou* **or** apply for an Exit-Entry Permit in China [S39].
- Local *hukou* registration rules may still ask about foreign citizenship (e.g., Fuzhou's declaration) [S42].

## 7.2 Consular-Treaty Travel Facilitation

The previous version of this section described "pro forma visas" placed in Chinese passports. No Chinese government source for that practice was found, so the section now covers the treaty provisions that do exist.

- **Which countries**: China's consular treaties with the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand each contain a clause under which both sides facilitate travel between the two countries for persons who claim both nationalities, without recognizing dual nationality [S25].
- **United States**: the supplementary exchange of notes to the China–US Consular Treaty (17 September 1980) states that this facilitation *"并不意味着两国政府承认双重国籍"* (does not mean either government recognizes dual nationality). Exit procedures and documents follow the law of the country of residence; entry procedures and documents follow the law of the destination country [S7].
- **What it does not do**: the clause facilitates travel. It does not create recognized dual nationality, and it does not replace the Chinese document rules in Parts Four and Five.
- **Other countries**: no comparable clause was found (e.g., France). Families with other nationalities should follow Parts Four and Five.

## 7.3 Pro Forma Visas and Right-of-Abode Certificates

This section covers the documents the **other** country can put in a Chinese passport so that Chinese exit control sees permission to enter the destination. No Chinese source describes any of this; the material below comes from US and UK government rules, a US immigration law firm, and practice reports.

- **Principle**: countries normally do not issue visas to their own citizens. US rules prohibit issuing a visa or other documentation to a US citizen for entry into the United States as a noncitizen [S63].
- **United States**: a US immigration law firm reports that US posts in China issue a "pro forma" visa in the Chinese passport of a US–China dual-national **minor**, as a courtesy and solely to let the child leave China for the US; the child still enters the US on the US passport. Adults are generally not considered. The practice is not in US regulations or the public Foreign Affairs Manual; since 2024 such visas have reportedly been issued as 10-year multiple-entry visas that look like ordinary B1/B2 visas [S65]. **[Practice report]** Not every post issues them, and applications are not always approved.
- **France**: **[Practice report] (second-hand)** French consulates are reported to have issued comparable visas to Franco-Chinese minors, and not to issue them after 18, so that from then on an Exit-Entry Permit or Travel Document plus the French passport is needed. No French government source and no first-hand account confirm this; France Diplomatie's China page does not mention the practice [S64]. Ask the French consulate handling your district before relying on it.
- **Chinese side**: the Chinese passport carrying the pro forma visa is used to leave and re-enter China (see 4.5). No Chinese source was found on how border inspection treats these visas.

**United Kingdom: certificate of entitlement to the right of abode.** Where a pro forma visa is a discretionary courtesy, the UK has a published equivalent that can sometimes be used the same way [S66]:

- **What it is**: official proof that the holder has the right of abode in the UK. It is not a visa and does not expire.
- **Who can apply**: a British citizen holding a foreign passport (or a Commonwealth citizen with the right of abode). It cannot be held together with a valid British passport — an existing British passport has to be surrendered.
- **Cost and time**: £589; roughly 3 weeks after the appointment when applying from outside the UK, about 8 weeks inside the UK.
- **Format change**: certificates are now issued **digitally** through a UKVI account rather than as a sticker in the foreign passport. An existing sticker (vignette) may still be used until that passport expires, and can be converted to digital for free.
- **Caveat for use at Chinese exit control**: the value of this route for leaving China rested on the sticker being physically visible in the Chinese passport. Whether a digital certificate shown as a share code satisfies Chinese border inspection or an airline check-in desk is **not established** — no Chinese source addresses it. Anyone planning to rely on it should test it with the airline and the local exit-entry authority (12367) before booking, and give up a British passport only after confirming this works.

## 7.4 Foreign Documents: Legalization and Apostille

- Since **7 November 2023**, public documents from other Apostille Convention member states need only an **apostille** from the issuing country for use in mainland China; legalization by the Chinese embassy/consulate is no longer required for them. Documents from non-member countries still go through consular legalization [S19].
- *Hukou* offices accept the foreign birth certificate with consular legalization, an apostille or a notarial certificate, plus a translation by a qualified agency (Fuzhou) [S42]. Dongguan requires foreign documents for the Exit-Entry Permit to be authenticated and translated by a qualified agency [S37].

## 7.5 What About DNA Testing for Hukou Registration?

- **Tianjin, children born abroad**: a parentage (DNA) test certificate is required for ages 6 to under 18, with review up to the district sub-bureau (quoted in 3.2) [S40].
- **Tianjin, children of overseas Chinese (华侨)**: ages 6 to under 18 are also checked against the national anti-trafficking DNA database, unless parentage has been confirmed by DNA testing [S41].
- **Shanghai**: no age-based requirement; a parentage report is needed only in listed cases (see 3.2) [S46][S47].
- Other cities set their own rules.
- **[Unverified]** "DNA testing institutions must be registered in the 国家司法鉴定名录网 (National Judicial Authentication Directory)." This was stated in the previous version; no source was found.

## 7.6 Children Registered as Foreigners (Residence Permits)

**[Practice report] Shanghai, former practice**: Shanghai is reported to have issued China-born dual-national children a dependent-type residence permit in the foreign passport, following the foreign parent. At 18 the young person had to switch to a study-based permit, usually arranged through an international school; after graduation they had to leave China or enrol again (e.g., at a local university or in a Chinese-language programme) to stay. A family-reunion (Q1) visa from a Chinese consulate abroad should also be possible.

**The period is not established.** No start or end date for this practice could be confirmed, and no official Shanghai document describing it was found. It also sits awkwardly with the 2008 Shanghai Public Security Bureau answer below, which treated a Shanghai-born child of one Chinese parent holding a foreign passport as Chinese [S50]. Anyone currently holding such a permit should confirm their position with the Shanghai exit-entry authority (12367) rather than assume the arrangement continues.

**What the official rules say**:

- Residence permits are for foreigners; the issuing office must confirm that the applicant has foreign nationality [S54].
- Private-affairs (私人事务类) permits cover the spouse, parents, parents-in-law and **under-18 children** of foreigners residing in China for work or study, for the same period as that family member's permit [S54][S55]. The child category ends at 18.
- Study (学习类) permits require a letter from the school and run for the study period [S54].
- Family reunion (Q1 visa, 团聚类 permit) covers family members of Chinese citizens—including adult children—with an invitation from the Chinese family member living in China [S55][S56]. Permits for under-18s run at most 3 years and not beyond the 18th birthday; others up to 2 years [S54].
- In 2008 the Shanghai Public Security Bureau answered that a Shanghai-born child with one Chinese parent who had obtained a foreign passport from a foreign consulate in China has Chinese nationality and should follow the nationality-conflict exit procedure [S50].
- A 2017 French Senate question reported that Franco-Chinese children born in China could no longer renew their visas because China regarded them as Chinese [S62].
- Being known to Chinese systems as a foreigner does not settle nationality: authorities determine nationality when the person applies for documents [S11][S12]. See 5.7 on visa-free entry.

# Part Eight: Summary and Quick Reference

## 8.1 Document Decision Tree

```
1. Does the person have Chinese nationality?
   (decided by the Chinese embassy/consulate abroad, or by the
    prefecture-level exit-entry office in China – see 1.3 and 3.3)
   ├── NO  → Foreign passport + Chinese visa (or other entry permission for foreigners)
   └── YES → go to 2

2. Where is the person now?
   ├── OUTSIDE China
   │     ├── Holds a valid Chinese passport → use it (see 4.2)
   │     └── Otherwise → Travel Document (旅行证) from a Chinese embassy/consulate
   │                     (first issue: usually the post in the country of birth)
   └── INSIDE China
         ├── Holds a valid Travel Document → leave on it (multi-entry within validity)
         ├── Has hukou + a valid Chinese passport → passport; leaving for the other-nationality
         │     country usually still needs an Exit-Entry Permit or a pro forma visa (see 4.5, 7.3)
         └── Otherwise → Exit-Entry Permit (出入境通行证): 3 months, one exit + one entry,
                         from the prefecture-level exit-entry office where you live
```

## 8.3 Key Takeaways

1. **China does not recognize dual nationality** for Chinese citizens. If your child is Chinese under Chinese law, that is the only nationality China recognizes [S1][S12].

2. **The parents' status at the time of birth decides.** For a child born abroad with a foreign nationality at birth: if **either** Chinese parent held foreign permanent residency at the birth, the child is not Chinese; if no Chinese parent did, the child is Chinese [S6][S21][S23].

3. **Nationality-conflict persons cannot get Chinese visas.** The consulate decides between visa and Travel Document; parents cannot choose [S20][S33].

4. **The document depends on location and what the person already holds** (see 8.1; trade-offs in 4.5). A person in China with a valid Travel Document simply leaves on it [S10].

5. **The Exit-Entry Permit is valid for 3 months for one exit and one entry** [S5]. Staying abroad longer means applying for a Travel Document there [S23].

6. **Turning 18 does not change the status automatically.** Nationality is determined when documents are applied for, and consular practice varies, especially if the family later settles abroad [S11][S20][S22].

7. **Hukou and the Exit-Entry Permit can coexist** [S36][S39], but local *hukou* rules vary (e.g., Fuzhou's declaration on foreign citizenship) [S42].

8. **Treaty-based travel facilitation exists only with some countries** (US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand) [S25][S7]. Pro forma visas (US; reportedly France) are discretionary and for minors; the UK's right-of-abode certificate is a published alternative (7.3). Visa-free entry with the foreign passport should not be assumed [S3][S34].

9. **Always check with local authorities.** Rules vary by province, city, consulate and port. Consult the local public security exit-entry office (in China) or the Chinese embassy/consulate for your district (abroad).
